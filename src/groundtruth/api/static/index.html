Retrace
How it works, in detail

Can this photo be trusted as evidence?

Insurance claims are settled on photographs, and nobody can inspect thousands a day by eye. Retrace runs six independent checks on a single image and reports what each one could and could not establish. It is given one photo and nothing else. No original, no before-and-after.

What it is

The job

Sort a pile of claim photos into three bins: fine, suspicious, and a person needs to look at this. It never decides a claim. It decides what order a human should work in, which is the thing that actually saves time.

Three outcomes rather than two, because a system forced to choose between "fine" and "fraud" on every image will be confidently wrong in public.

How it decides

Six checks run independently, each answering a different question, each returning three separate things: can I speak to this image at all, what did I find, and how much do I trust myself here.

A check that cannot run is dropped from the vote rather than counted as neutral. "I have nothing to say about this" and "I looked and found nothing" are different claims, and averaging them together is how these systems produce confident nonsense.

The unusual part

Most of the work here went into finding out where it stops working, and those limits are published rather than buried.

Four detectors were built, measured, and switched off because the measurement said they did not work. One of them scored below chance after a repair. The checks that ship are the ones that survived.

What it will not do. It does not catch AI-generated images from the pixels; that only works when the file carries a signature, and one re-save removes it. It misses a copied region smaller than about 1% of the frame, one blended with a heavy feather, or one that was mirrored on its way in. Those numbers come from deliberately trying to defeat it, and every one of them is written down.

Example 1. Something was copied inside the photo

The most common way to inflate a claim is to copy part of the photo over something else: extend the damage onto a panel that was never hit, or paint clean surface over a scratch. Retrace catches this by finding two areas of one photo that are the same area twice.

1. What was handed in A car submitted with a claim. The front and rear doors are both crumpled. You have no original to check it against, and neither does Retrace.
2. What Retrace flagged It marked two areas of bodywork, saying they are the same damage appearing twice, 750 pixels apart. That is the claim. The next panel is the proof.
3. The proof, side by side The two areas, cut out and zoomed. Every crease and highlight matches, because one is the other. Measured, they differ 73× less than two genuinely different parts of the same photograph. Two real dents are never identical. A camera cannot photograph the same damage twice in one frame.
4. The machine's own view How suspicious each part of the photo is. Black is fine, orange and white are suspicious. Two bright patches, because a copy always leaves two marks: where it came from and where it went.
Was it actually right? Show me the real photo

In the genuine photograph only the front door is crumpled. The rear panel damage was pasted in. Retrace never saw this image, it worked entirely from the edited one. This is here so you can mark its homework rather than take our word for it.

Example 2. The editor left the original inside the file

A different photo, and a completely different route to the answer. Most image files quietly carry a second, postage-stamp-sized copy of themselves, the thumbnail your file browser shows. Editing software often rewrites the main picture and forgets to update that thumbnail, which leaves the original sitting inside the same file.

1. What was handed in A subsidence claim. The crack runs the full height of the corner.
2. The thumbnail hidden in the file Pulled straight out of the same file. The crack is half the length. These are real pixels from before the edit, not a guess or a reconstruction.
3. What changed between them The two versions compared. Same colour code: black is unchanged, bright is changed. The duplicated section lights up, 3.1% of the picture.

This one is luck, not skill, and it is worth being clear about that. It only works when the editor forgot the thumbnail. Most files carry no usable copy, and across the 224 photographs Retrace is scored on, this shortcut was available zero times. None of its measured accuracy comes from it. Example 1 is how it normally has to work.
